# Contour Kinetics

**Philosophy:** Movement made legible by the land it crosses. A survey of energy.

Contour Kinetics treats the picture plane as a topographic field — a terrain sheet drawn by
someone who has walked every ridge of it. Concentric lines accumulate the way sediment does,
patiently, thousands of thin strokes describing a single swelling landform. Nothing is loud on
its own; meaning arrives from density. Where the rings crowd together the ground is steep and the
eye is pulled; where they open out the composition breathes. The surface should read as an
instrument reading — cartographic, exact, quietly obsessive — as though a lifetime of fieldwork
were compressed into one sheet and every line placed by hand with painstaking attention.

**Space and form.** The field is total: contours run edge to edge, unbroken by frames or boxes,
deformed by an invisible pressure so no ring is a perfect circle. Against this patient substrate a
single decisive gesture is allowed — one route, dashed, kinetic, cutting upward across the grain
of the land toward a summit where the rings clench into a knot. The route is the only thing in a
hurry. Everything else has been here for ten thousand years.

**Colour and material.** A palette held to four notes: a warm bone paper that looks sun-aged, a
deep warm ink close to black, one signal colour — a hot trail-orange reserved exclusively for the
route and its markers — and a single muted survey-green used only in the faintest contour weight.
No gradients that call attention to themselves; colour is used the way a cartographer uses it, to
classify, never to decorate. The restraint must look like a decision made by a master, not an
absence.

**Scale and rhythm.** Extreme contrast of type size: one word large enough to be read across a
street, set heavy and condensed, sitting inside the terrain like a landform label; everything else
dropped to small monospaced reference text — coordinates, grid codes, elevation ticks, a scale
bar — scattered with the confidence of marginalia on a working map. The rhythm is slow field,
fast route, silent white. Numbers are characters here, not data; they belong to the visual
language of the instrument.

**Composition and hierarchy.** Read along the natural optical path and let the terminal corner
carry the payload. The summit anchors the upper field; the route descends from it to meet the
reader at the lower edge, where the sheet becomes perforated — a working document meant to be
torn. Hierarchy is built by contour density and by the one accent colour, never by rules or
shadows. Generous margins; nothing touches an edge except the contours themselves, which are meant
to run off as though the land continues.

**Craftsmanship.** The sheet must look like the product of countless unhurried hours — every
contour individually weighted, every label kerned by eye, the route hand-plotted point by point,
the whole thing carrying the authority of someone at the very top of the discipline of drawing
terrain. It should feel discovered rather than designed: an artifact recovered from a survey
office, proof that something as fleeting as a chase across a neighbourhood can be mapped,
measured, and understood.
