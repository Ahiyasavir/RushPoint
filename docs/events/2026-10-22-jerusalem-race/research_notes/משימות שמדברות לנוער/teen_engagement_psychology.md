# Teen engagement psychology: what makes a challenge meaningful (not silly) for 15-18 year olds, applied to missions in a competitive night city race

Research notes, 2026-10-19. Scope: the psychology of motivation in adolescent group challenges and its translation into mission design rules. Every principle below ends in "how to use it in a mission" and "how it fails" (in the Inferences subsections, because they are my application of the findings, not findings themselves).

Source-quality caveat for the whole file: PMC (pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) returned a browser-verification wall on every fetch, so the full texts of Yeager, Dahl & Dweck 2018 and Bryan, Yeager et al. 2016 were NOT read. Their claims below come from abstracts, journal pages, university fact sheets and press releases. Scott Nicholson's escape-room white paper downloaded only as unreadable binary; its claims come from secondary summaries.

## 1. Status, respect and autonomy (Self-Determination Theory; Yeager's status-and-respect account)

### Takeaway
Adolescents are not less motivated than children, they are motivated by different things: being accorded status and respect, and autonomy from adult control. Interventions that lecture or talk down lose effect with age, while those that frame the desired behaviour as what a respected, independent, older person would choose work. Of the three SDT needs, competence was the strongest predictor of self-determined motivation in a 144-study meta-analysis, then autonomy, then relatedness.

### Cited Findings
- Yeager, Dahl & Dweck (2018, *Perspectives on Psychological Science*) ask why school-based preventive interventions work reasonably well for children but less so for middle adolescents, and argue that they fail when they do not align with adolescents' heightened desire to feel respected and be accorded status. — [APS journal page](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/perspectives/1745691617722620/); [UCLA Center for the Developing Adolescent summary](https://developingadolescent.semel.ucla.edu/topics/item/why-interventions-to-influence-adolescent-behavior-often-fail-but-could-succeed)
- The same paper reviews three families of interventions that do work: ones that directly harness the desire for status and respect, ones that give adolescents more respectful treatment from adults, and ones that lessen the threat of status loss. — [APS journal page](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/perspectives/1745691617722620/)
- A Yeager et al. 2015 meta-analysis of anti-bullying programs (as reported secondarily) found effectiveness declined with age, reaching a null effect in adolescence. — [eScholarship PDF](https://cloudfront.escholarship.org/dist/prd/content/qt9mm4q4fb/qt9mm4q4fb.pdf?t=p2bcou) (secondary report; not checked against the 2015 meta-analysis itself)
- Bryan, Yeager et al. (2016, *PNAS*, doi 10.1073/pnas.1604586113): a double-blind randomized placebo-controlled experiment with 8th graders framed healthy eating as consistent with adolescent values of autonomy from adult control and social justice, by exposing food-marketing executives' manipulative tactics. Compared with standard health education, it made healthy eating look more autonomy-assertive, and students chose fewer sugary snacks and drinks a day later in an unrelated context, without knowing they were tracked. Effects were described as modest but significant. — [Chicago Booth press release](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/media-relations-and-communications/press-releases/broccoli-v-french-fries-study-finds-appealing-teens-impulse-rebel-can-curb-unhealthy); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2016/09/160912173844.htm); [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/teens-reject-junk-food-when-healthy-eating-framed-rebellion). Sample/setting details conflict across press reports (suburban vs rural Texas school; "over 500" students).
- The UCLA STEPS fact sheet summarizes the same study as replacing lectures with articles exposing deceptive marketing, and conveying that higher-status (older) students were choosing to eat healthier. — [STEPS fact sheet: Respected Role](https://developingadolescent.semel.ucla.edu/assets/uploads/research/resources/STEPS_FactSheet_RespectedRole.pdf)
- Yeager's "mentor mindset" (book *10 to 25*) pairs high expectations with high support, contrasted with the "enforcer" (rules, low support), the "protector" (lowers standards to shield young people) and the "disconnector". His account describes sensitivity to status and respect as extending into the mid-twenties. — [UT Texas Connect, 2025](https://texasconnect.utexas.edu/2025/09/29/psychologist-david-yeager-explains-how-adults-can-best-get-through-to-young-people/); [Progress-focused book summary](https://www.progressfocused.com/2024/11/david-yeagers-book-10-to-25-about.html)
- "Wise feedback": criticism framed as reflecting high standards plus belief the young person can meet them increases willingness to improve. — [Progress-focused summary](https://www.progressfocused.com/2024/11/david-yeagers-book-10-to-25-about.html) (secondary)
- Bureau, Howard et al. (2022, *Review of Educational Research*, 144 studies, >79,000 students): teacher autonomy support predicted need satisfaction and self-determined motivation more strongly than parental support; competence was the strongest predictor of self-determined motivation, followed by autonomy, then relatedness. Autonomy-supportive behaviours named: empathy, giving explanatory rationales, offering meaningful choices. — [PMC8935530](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8935530/); [SDT.org PDF](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/2021_PathwaystoStudentMotiv_BureaHowardEtal.pdf)

### Inferences
Design rules derived from the above (my application, not tested in a race context):

**Rule 1.1, treat them as the respected expert, not the pupil.**
- How to use it: write missions in the voice of a peer or a client briefing a capable crew ("We need a team that can..."), give real responsibility (e.g. "you are the only team that will see this clue, the next team depends on what you report"), and use high standards stated openly ("most adult teams fail this one in under 10 minutes").
- How it fails: a cheerful adult-to-child tone, exclamation-mark enthusiasm, explaining things they obviously know, rules justified by "because we said so". The "protector" failure: making it easy so nobody feels bad. Over-scaffolded hints that arrive before anyone asked.

**Rule 1.2, give real choices that change the outcome.**
- How to use it: two or three missions open at once with visible point values and difficulty, so the team picks its own risk; a route decision (long safe path vs short hard puzzle); a "bank it or gamble it" choice on bonus points. Say WHY a rule exists (rationale is one of the three autonomy-support behaviours in the meta-analysis), especially safety rules.
- How it fails: fake choice (both options lead to the same place), choices with no information to decide on, or a linear chain dressed as a menu. Unexplained restrictions read as control and invite the "rebel" response the 2016 study harnessed.

**Rule 1.3, frame the mission against an outside adversary or an adult system, not against the players.**
- How to use it: the Bryan/Yeager lever is "resisting manipulation by powerful adults". A narrative where teams expose a cover-up, outwit "the organizers" or crack something hidden from the public channels the same value. Make the organizer the antagonist players get to beat.
- How it fails: when the "adversary" is the players' own dignity (dares, humiliating tasks), or when the rebellion frame is used to push something unsafe at night.

**Rule 1.4, competence first.** The meta-analysis ranks competence as the top predictor; missions should make the team visibly good at something (see Section 2).

### Gaps
- Full text of Yeager, Dahl & Dweck 2018 not accessed (PMC verification wall); the specific "hidden status" interventions it reviews are not verified here beyond the abstract.
- I could not locate the PNAS 2019 "respect" paper the brief mentions; I found no source confirming a 2019 PNAS study by that description, so it is not cited.
- No study found that tests status/respect framing in recreational games or races; the transfer is an inference.
- Exact age range: most cited evidence is 8th grade (13-14); evidence specific to 15-18 is thinner, though Yeager's account claims the sensitivity extends to the mid-twenties.

## 2. Challenge-skill balance, flow, "hard fun" and desirable difficulty

### Takeaway
Flow appears when challenge and skill are BOTH high and in balance; too hard yields anxiety, too easy yields apathy or relaxation. Papert's "hard fun" says people enjoy work because it is hard, provided it is hard in the right way for them. Bjork's desirable difficulties hold only when the learner has enough cues to succeed, otherwise the difficulty becomes undesirable.

### Cited Findings
- Flow requires perceived challenges in balance with perceived skills; later revisions specify flow appears only when both are high (above the person's mean). — [Engeser & Rheinberg 2008, Motivation and Emotion (PDF)](https://www.uni-trier.de/fileadmin/fb1/prof/PSY/PGA/bilder/Engeser___Rheinberg_2008.pdf)
- The boredom quadrant was later renamed "relaxation" because expected differences did not appear consistently; the exact form of the balance is still debated. — [Engeser & Rheinberg 2008](https://www.uni-trier.de/fileadmin/fb1/prof/PSY/PGA/bilder/Engeser___Rheinberg_2008.pdf); [Springer 2024 board-game flow study](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10902-024-00846-4)
- A US experience-sampling study of 828 teenagers (paged 8 times a day for a week) operationalized flow as challenge and skill both above the sample mean. — [World Database of Happiness entry, Csikszentmihalyi & Hunter 2003](https://worlddatabaseofhappiness-archive.eur.nl/correlational-findings/14995/) (details should be checked against the original)
- In classroom flow research, tasks demanding more than a student can handle produce anxiety and discouragement; low challenge with low skill leads toward apathy. — [Redalyc educational flow article](https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/3311/331131296002.pdf)
- *Talented Teenagers* (Csikszentmihalyi et al.) found enjoyment crucial for teens becoming and staying interested in a talent area. — [Hosei University repository (review)](https://hosei.ecats-library.jp/da/repository/00005505/jhs_2004Asakawa_repo.pdf)
- Papert, "Hard Fun" (2002): a child described Logo programming as fun BECAUSE it was hard; Papert's conclusion is that everyone likes hard, challenging things, but the challenge must suit the individual. — [Daily Papert, "Hard Fun"](https://dailypapert.com/hard-fun/)
- Bjork's desirable difficulties include generation (producing an answer rather than being given it), spacing, interleaving, varied conditions and testing; generation helps only if the learner has enough prior knowledge and cues to succeed. — [Bjork lab chapter PDF](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/RBjork_inpress.pdf); [Wikipedia: Desirable difficulty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desirable_difficulty) (overview)

### Inferences
**Rule 2.1, overestimate them, then catch them.** Target the upper edge: a mission a strong team solves in ~5-8 minutes with real effort. Provide a hint ladder that costs points, so the team chooses when to drop the difficulty (keeps autonomy, prevents anxiety).
- How to use it: tiered missions (bronze/silver/gold point values on the same station), escalating difficulty across the night as skill grows, missions that use what the team learned earlier in the race (interleaving).
- How it fails: (a) overly easy tasks ("take a photo with a tree") read as childish and land in the apathy quadrant; (b) impossible tasks with no hint path produce anxiety and the team quits or cheats; (c) the same mechanic repeated all night collapses challenge as skill rises.

**Rule 2.2, let them generate the answer.** "Figure out where the next point is" beats "go to the address printed here". The answer should be produced by the team, not handed over.
- How it fails: generation with no cues (outside knowledge they cannot have, a reference only adults get) is the "undesirable difficulty" Bjork warns about.

**Rule 2.3, physical hard is fine, social hard is risky.** Hard fun applies to puzzles, navigation, coordination and endurance. Making the HARD part "do something embarrassing in public" is a different challenge (see Section 5).

### Gaps
- No flow study specific to timed team street games for 15-18 year olds was found.
- No data on what solve time teen teams actually prefer per mission; the 5-minute ceiling below (Section 6) is from escape-room practitioners, not research.

## 3. Curiosity gaps, mystery and reveals (Loewenstein information-gap theory)

### Takeaway
Curiosity is triggered when attention is focused on a specific gap in one's knowledge; it requires some prior knowledge (no one is curious about something they know nothing about), it is intense but transient, and it tends to disappoint once satisfied. So a mystery must be specific, partially known, and paid off quickly with something better than expected.

### Cited Findings
- Loewenstein (1994, *Psychological Bulletin* 116(1):75-98) defines curiosity as cognitively induced deprivation arising from a perceived gap in knowledge, appearing when attention becomes focused on that gap. — [Stafforini bibliographic page](https://stafforini.com/works/loewenstein-1994-psychology-curiosity-review/)
- The theory explains curiosity's intensity, transience, association with impulsivity and tendency to disappoint when satisfied. — [Stafforini](https://stafforini.com/works/loewenstein-1994-psychology-curiosity-review/)
- Curiosity needs some prior knowledge: people are not curious about things they know absolutely nothing about. — [Nautilus, "Curiosity depends on what you already know"](https://nautil.us/curiosity-depends-on-what-you-already-know-235803)
- Some researchers dispute framing curiosity as aversive, though the knowledge-gap concept is widely used. — [Springer review, Educational Psychology Review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09497-x)

### Inferences
**Rule 3.1, reveal partial information, name the gap.** A mission card that shows 3 of 5 pieces ("you have the street and the year, find the name") outperforms a fully open question or a fully hidden one.
- How to use it: a night-long meta-mystery revealed one fragment per mission (each completion unlocks a piece); sealed envelopes or locked missions whose title is visible but content is not (RushPoint already supports hidden missions with a search area); a final reveal that recontextualizes earlier clues.
- How it fails: (a) the gap is vague ("there's a secret!") with no anchor, so no deprivation is felt; (b) the payoff is weaker than the build-up (Loewenstein's "disappoints when satisfied") — a big mystery whose answer is "the sponsor's logo" breeds cynicism; (c) the gap is held open too long — curiosity is transient, so a clue that only pays off two hours later is forgotten.

**Rule 3.2, the city itself as the mystery.** Real, verifiable hidden facts about the place they are standing in (a bullet mark, an inscription, a forgotten story) give prior knowledge plus a gap, and are adult-grade content.
- How it fails: trivia that could be googled in 10 seconds is not a gap, it is a chore.

### Gaps
- No study on curiosity gaps specifically in location-based games or with teen teams was found.

## 4. Competition vs cooperation, and rivalry

### Takeaway
Competition BETWEEN groups raises cooperation WITHIN them, even mere comparison with another group does. Rivalry (a competitive relationship with history, similarity and close contests) raises motivation beyond the objective stakes, e.g. runners ran about 5 seconds per km faster when a rival was present. But low performers can disengage when shown their rank, and peers sharply increase teen risk-taking — the critical safety constraint for a night race.

### Cited Findings
- Böhm & Rockenbach: mere comparison to another structurally independent group increases intra-group cooperation and efficiency; real inter-group conflict also increases intra-group cooperation but at a cost to collective efficiency. — [PLoS One / PMC3566068](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3566068/)
- Reuben & Tyran: "all-can-win" competition (every group can rank first, e.g. thresholds rather than a single winner) produced a universal increase in cooperation. — [Columbia working paper PDF](https://drupalgsb-test.paas.cc.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/3424/AllCanWin.pdf)
- Cárdenas & Mantilla (as summarized): individuals with low relative performance reduced contributions after feedback, while low-performing groups increased theirs. — [search summary of related work, Frontiers/PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4330795) (secondary; not verified in full text)
- Kilduff, Elfenbein & Staw (2010, *Academy of Management Journal* 53(5):943-969): rivalry is a subjective competitive relationship that raises psychological involvement beyond objective stakes; it is predicted by similarity, proximity and prior competitive interactions (NCAA basketball data). — [SUFE academic newsletter abstract](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/361581); [CUHK seminar page](https://www.bschool.cuhk.edu.hk/events/the-psychology-of-rivalry-a-relationally-dependent-analysis-of-competition/)
- Kilduff (2014, *Social Psychological and Personality Science*): six years of archival running-club data showed a rival's presence made runners about 4.92 seconds/km faster; predictors were similarity, repeated competition and close contests. — [APS "The Upside of Rivalry"](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/minds-business/the-upside-of-rivalry-higher-motivation-better-performance.html); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/07/140702122438.htm). Sample size reported inconsistently (82 runners/112 races vs 184 races).
- Gardner & Steinberg (2005): in a driving game, adolescents (13-16) and youths (18-22) took more risks in the presence of peers; the effect was exaggerated for them relative to adults. — [Frontiers in Psychology 2014 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01476/full); [LiveScience](https://www.livescience.com/11668-friends-drive-friends-risks.html). Peer group size (2 vs 3) differs between sources.

### Inferences
**Rule 4.1, make the competition between teams, the cooperation inside.** A live leaderboard and visible comparison with other teams is cheap fuel. Missions that require every member (roles, split-up information, one person holds the map, another the code) turn inter-team pressure into intra-team cohesion.
- How it fails: missions solvable by one strong member leave the others as spectators; intra-team scoring (individual MVPs) splits the group.

**Rule 4.2, engineer rivalries.** Pair teams that are similar (same school/year), let them meet repeatedly (shared stations, head-to-head flash missions), and keep scores close (catch-up mechanics, hidden final multipliers).
- How to use it: "duel" missions where two specific teams race for one prize, named rivalry announcements, a mid-race standings reveal when it is still close.
- How it fails: a runaway leader kills rivalry for everyone below (close contests are a rivalry predictor); losing teams that see a hopeless rank disengage (Cárdenas & Mantilla). Mitigate with "all-can-win" thresholds (badges for any team crossing a score), and per-segment or per-category winners.

**Rule 4.3, never reward speed or daring that maps onto physical risk.** Peer presence amplifies teen risk-taking, and a night city race adds traffic, darkness and pressure. Speed should be won by thinking, not by running across roads.
- How it fails: time bonuses for crossing distance, dares involving heights, roads, strangers' property or trespass; "first team wins" missions at a location reached by a busy crossing.

### Gaps
- No study found on rivalry or inter-group competition specifically among 15-18 year olds in recreational settings.
- No evidence found on whether live leaderboards help or hurt the bottom teams in street games specifically.

## 5. Pride vs embarrassment: what teens want to post, and when being watched hurts

### Takeaway
Self-conscious emotion from being watched by a peer peaks in adolescence (embarrassment, arousal and medial prefrontal activity all peak in teens versus children and adults). Many teens feel pressure to post only what makes them look good (about 43% in a 2018 Pew survey). So a public-facing mission is a bet on the teen's image: it pays when the output displays competence, belonging or style, and it costs when it makes them look foolish without a payoff.

### Cited Findings
- Somerville et al. (2013, *Psychological Science*, 69 participants aged 8 to ~23): believing a peer was watching them via camera induced self-conscious emotion stronger in adolescents than in children and adults; embarrassment, autonomic arousal and MPFC activation converged and peaked in adolescence, with greater MPFC-striatum connectivity in teens. — [APS news release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/?p=87345); [LiveScience](https://livescience.com/37910-self-consciousness-teenage-brain.html)
- Somerville's 2013 review (*Current Directions in Psychological Science*) argues adolescents are especially attuned to social evaluation, partly due to ongoing brain development. — [SAGE journal reader](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/reader/10.1177/0963721413476512)
- Pew 2018 (teens 13-17): ~43% feel pressure to post only content that makes them look good; ~37% feel pressure to post things that will get lots of likes or comments; 81% say social media makes them feel more connected to friends. — [NPR, 2021](https://www.npr.org/2021/10/05/1043194385/whistleblowers-testimony-facebook-instagram) (secondary reporting of Pew; Pew report itself not accessed)
- Teens reportedly delete posts that get weak engagement and curate accounts to a consistent "aesthetic". — [Ad Council](https://adcouncil.org/finstas-party-accounts-how-gen-z-is-using-instagram) (marketing source, anecdotal)

### Inferences
**Rule 5.1, the "would they post it?" test.** A public mission's output should be something a 16-year-old would choose to put on their own story: a striking night photo, a team achievement, a cracked code, an impressive location, a rare access ("we got onto the roof of X with the guide"), a high-quality shot. Competence and exclusivity are postable; slapstick is not.
- How it fails: "sing a song to a stranger", "dance in the square", costume humiliation, the "troll" style mission where the team is the joke. That is embarrassment without payoff, felt at the age it peaks.

**Rule 5.2, if there is exposure, it must be chosen, collective and paid.** Performative missions can work when (a) optional with a high point reward (autonomy), (b) done as a whole team (diffuses individual exposure), (c) framed as bold/brave rather than silly, and (d) the result looks good.
- How it fails: forced fun — mandatory performance, singling out one member, staff filming the reluctant one.

**Rule 5.3, design the artefact.** Give them something worth sharing: a team "proof" image the app generates with their time and rank, a finish card, a final photo spot designed to look great at night (RushPoint already has canvas-drawn story cards).
- How it fails: a cheap-looking, logo-heavy share card is the kind of post the curation behaviour above deletes.

### Gaps
- No study found specifically on which game/challenge content teens post vs. hide; the "would they post it" test is an inference from self-presentation and evaluation-sensitivity findings.
- Pew figures are via secondary press; the original report was not accessed.

## 6. Escape-room and puzzle-hunt principles that produce "aha"

### Takeaway
Practitioner guidance converges: puzzles must be fair (all needed information present, no outside knowledge, no red herrings), have one self-validating answer, clear links between clue and puzzle, consistent rules and feedback, and a mechanism not obvious at first glance that reveals itself as the team works. Hard is allowed; unfair is not.

### Cited Findings
- Nicholson's 2015 white paper surveyed 175 escape-room facilities worldwide on themes, player demographics, room features and design patterns. — [Meaningful Play 2016 abstract](https://meaningfulplay.msu.edu/proceedings2016/abstract.php?paperid=14); [Hexagram overview](https://tag.hexagram.ca/can-you-get-out-an-overview-of-escape-rooms/). Its sampling was noted as not fully representative. — [Univie repository](https://phaidra.univie.ac.at/download/o:2131778)
- Puzzle categories credited to Nicholson include symbol substitution with a key, shape manipulation, riddles/logic puzzles and light-based puzzles (black light, darkness). — [2017 Escape Room Enthusiast Survey PDF](https://thecodex.ca/wp-content/uploads/2017/09/2017EscapeRoomEnthusiastSurveyV1.1.pdf)
- "13 rules for escape room puzzle design": be fair (designer on the player's side, which does not mean easy); clue everything; one answer; self-validating answer (players know they are right before entering it); clear clue-puzzle links; "aha" correlations must follow real-world logic; a puzzle should take under ~5 minutes for someone who knows the method; tedious work must not be ambiguous; no destroyable states; give feedback; be consistent; beta test because puzzles will be too hard; avoid red herrings and outside knowledge. — [The Codex, 13 rules](https://thecodex.ca/13-rules-for-escape-room-puzzle-design/)
- Puzzled Pint guidelines: an ideal puzzle has a mechanism not obvious at first glance that is revealed as the solver works on it. — [Puzzled Pint guidelines](https://puzzledpint.com/?p=1079)
- MIT-style/Mathcamp hunt rules state the hunt is supposed to be fun while warning that some puzzles are very hard. — [Mathcamp hunt guide](https://hunt.mathcamp.org/puzzles/2005/guide.html)

### Inferences
**Rule 6.1, the aha needs a hidden mechanism plus a self-check.** Design each puzzle mission around one twist the team discovers (e.g. the numbers on the card are street numbers on the street they're on), and an answer that clicks so they know it's right before submitting (it spells a word, points at a real place).
- How it fails: answers that only the designer's brain would reach, "guess what we meant", trial-and-error code entry, wrong-answer penalties for a puzzle that was ambiguous. Each one turns "aha" into "that's stupid", which is lethal to adult credibility with teens.

**Rule 6.2, beta test with real teens at night.** Puzzles will be too hard (rule 12); darkness, cold, phone battery and noise make it harder still. Test on site at the same hour.
- How it fails: designing at a desk in daylight; inscriptions that cannot be read at 21:00 without a flashlight.

**Rule 6.3, use the night.** Light-based mechanisms (UV markings, flashlight-revealed clues, city lights as signals) are a recognised escape-room category and are native to a night race.

### Gaps
- Nicholson's full white paper text was not readable (binary PDF); figures on puzzle frequencies are not reported here.
- No authoritative, peer-reviewed puzzle-hunt design guide was found; the "13 rules" and Puzzled Pint are practitioner sources.
- Kounios & Beeman insight neuroscience was surfaced only via a weak source and is not cited.

## Cross-cutting summary of failure modes (from the sections above)
These collect the "how it fails" items so a mission author can check each draft against them (inferences, sourced in the sections above):
- **Infantilizing** (Sec. 1): adult-to-child tone, over-explaining, protective easiness, unexplained rules.
- **Clowning** (Sec. 5): the team is the joke; humiliation as the challenge.
- **Forced fun** (Sec. 1, 5): no choice, mandatory performance, one member singled out.
- **Embarrassment without payoff** (Sec. 5): public exposure whose output they would not post.
- **Too easy** (Sec. 2): apathy quadrant, "photo with a tree", googleable trivia (Sec. 3).
- **Unfair-hard** (Sec. 2, 6): outside knowledge, ambiguity, no hint path.
- **Hollow mystery** (Sec. 3): vague gap, weak payoff, payoff too late.
- **Dead competition** (Sec. 4): runaway leader, hopeless bottom teams, one-person missions.
- **Risk rewarded** (Sec. 4): speed or daring that maps onto traffic, heights or trespass at night, amplified by peer presence.
