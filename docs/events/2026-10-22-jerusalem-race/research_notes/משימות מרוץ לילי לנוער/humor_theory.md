# Humor, weirdness and memorability: a design framework for teen night race missions with strangers

Scope note: 15 tool calls (searches only, no full-text fetches of the papers). Most findings come from the abstracts, the authors' own pages, or reputable press summaries of the primary papers. Where a figure came only from secondary coverage, it says so. Every "how to use / how it fails" line is an INFERENCE that applies the cited research to mission writing; none of them were tested in the sources.

## 1. Benign Violation Theory (McGraw & Warren): core findings, the boundaries, psychological distance

### Takeaway
Something is funny when it is a violation (something is "wrong") and benign (it is OK) at the same moment. Missions are funny when they break a small social norm in a way everyone can see is harmless. If the mission is too safe it is boring, if it is too threatening it is cruel, and "distance" (hypothetical, played as a role, not personal) is the dial between the two.

### Cited Findings
- McGraw & Warren (2010, *Psychological Science*, "Benign Violations: Making Immoral Behavior Funny"): humor occurs when and only when three conditions hold together: the situation is a violation, the situation is benign, and both are perceived at the same time. — [ScienceDaily summary of the paper](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2010/08/100809142042.htm); [McGraw's site](https://petermcgraw.org/?p=400)
- Across five experiments, morally wrong but harmless acts drew both amusement and disgust. They named three ways a violation becomes benign: (a) weak commitment to the norm being broken, (b) psychological distance from the violation, (c) another norm that makes the act acceptable (an alternative reading). — [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2010/08/100809142042.htm)
- McGraw, Warren, Williams & Leonard (2012, *Psychological Science*, "Too Close for Comfort, or Too Far to Care?"): how funny something is depends on two things together: how bad the violation is (a tragedy vs. a small mishap) and how far away the person feels from it. Severe violations need more distance, while small mishaps are funnier when they are close. Too much distance makes something tame and not funny. — [the paper's PDF](https://leeds-faculty.colorado.edu/mcgrawp/pdf/mcgraw.warren.williams.leonard.2012.pdf); [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/locating-sweet-spot-when-jokes-about-tragedy-are-seen-funny)
- Hurricane Sandy study: jokes were rated fairly funny before landfall, hit a low point about 15 days after it, peaked around day 36, and fell to a new low at day 99. Their revised version of "tragedy plus time" is that it takes time, "not too little yet not too much." Physical, social and hypothetical distance work the same way. — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/time-tragedy-violation-and-humor-psychologists-discover-a-comedic-sweet-spot/); [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/when-are-jokes-about-a-tragedy-funny/)
- McGraw has said in interviews that there is no algorithm for timing: it depends on the event, the audience and the person telling the joke. — [LiveScience](https://www.livescience.com/23062-joke-tragedy.html)
- Replications are mixed. A 2023 study supported the theory only in part: purity violations (the more benign ones) caused amusement, but there was no effect of psychological distance. Another team called the original method flawed and re-tested it. Treat BVT as a useful design lens, not a settled law. — [IRIS Bocconi listing](https://iris.unibocconi.it/handle/11565/3735443); [IRIS UniTN](https://iris.unitn.it/handle/11572/379027)

### Inferences (applied to missions)
- **Principle: violation + benign at the same time.**
  Use: every mission breaks one small social norm (a formal ceremony at a falafel stand, a slow-motion run across a crosswalk on green) and comes with a visible "safe" signal (team shirts, a staff card, an obvious game).
  Fails: with no violation (a "selfie by the fountain") nobody laughs; when the "benign" half is missing for the stranger (a stranger who cannot tell it is a game feels threatened), it turns into harassment.
- **Principle: the target of the violation matters more than its size.**
  Use: the violation lands on the TEAM's own dignity (they dress up, sing, act ridiculously), never on the stranger's.
  Fails: "go and convince a stranger they won something," or anything where the stranger is the joke, breaks the benign condition for the one person who did not agree to play.
- **Principle: weak commitment to the norm.** Pick norms nobody cares about deeply (the "right" way to order shawarma, how people walk, ceremony and formality). Fails: norms tied to religion, mourning, the IDF/security, the Western Wall or holy sites, or disability. In Jerusalem these are strong commitments, so they read as a real violation and not a benign one.
- **Principle: an alternative norm, "it's a game."** The mission card itself gives the stranger the other reading ("we're in a race, can you help us for 10 seconds?"). Fails: hidden-camera framing, where the stranger only finds out afterwards (if at all).
- **Principle: the distance dial.** A role or persona, a hypothetical, and a ridiculous made-up premise all add distance, so a slightly edgier violation can stay benign. Fails: making it personal (looks, weight, a stranger's accent) removes the distance, while making it too abstract ("tell a joke") removes the violation and kills the laugh.
- **Calibration test for the writer:** read the mission as (a) the team, (b) the stranger, (c) a parent watching the clip the next morning. All three must see it as "wrong but OK." If any of the three sees only "OK," make it stronger. If any sees "not OK," cut it.

### Gaps
- No study found that tests BVT directly on live, in-person tasks with strangers, or on teenagers specifically.
- I did not read the full 2010/2012 papers, so the effect sizes are not reported here.

## 2. Incongruity, absurd contrast, commitment to the bit, deadpan, and improv methods ("Yes, and", heightening, the UCB "game")

### Takeaway
The improv method that suits missions best is UCB's: set up a grounded "base reality," introduce ONE unusual thing, then heighten it by asking "if this is true, what else is true?" Humor comes from one clear absurd rule played completely seriously, not from piling up random craziness.

### Cited Findings
- In UCB-style improv, performers first set up a believable base reality, then find "the first unusual thing" (the first break from it). The game of the scene is the pattern of that unusual behavior. — [Hideout Theatre: "A UCB approach to scenework and Game"](https://www.hideouttheatre.com/?p=25077); [Joe Boyd, "Find the Game"](https://joeboyd.substack.com/p/feeling-stuck-find-the-game)
- The steps as taught: find the first unusual thing, define it so the partners and the audience recognize it, heighten it (repeat and escalate while staying inside the scene's logic), then play the pattern. — [Joe Boyd substack](https://joeboyd.substack.com/p/feeling-stuck-find-the-game); [CourseHorse UCB-style Improv 201 description](https://coursehorse.com/atlanta/classes/performing-arts/improv/impv-201-game-of-the-scene-and-advanced-game-technique1)
- The heightening tool is "If this unusual thing is true, then what else is true?" — [Bennington College course description](https://curriculum.bennington.edu/spring2022/2021/10/12/advanced-improvisation-the-game-of-the-scene/)
- The same tool is used for screenwriting and stand-up, so it travels outside the improv stage. — [ScriptMag](https://scriptmag.com/features/improvising-screenplays-finding-funny-screenplay-using-game-scene); [Funny How substack](https://funnyhow.substack.com/p/using-game-of-the-scene-in-standup)
- Caveat: none of these is an official UCB document, and wording differs between teachers. — (as above)

### Inferences (applied to missions)
- **Principle: base reality first.**
  Use: set the mission inside a real, everyday frame (a real kiosk, a real bus stop, a real line) so the one strange element stands out.
  Fails: missions that are absurd from start to end ("be a dinosaur") have no contrast, and read as childish rather than funny.
- **Principle: ONE unusual thing.**
  Use: one rule per mission ("everything you say to the cashier must be sung like an opera").
  Fails: three rules at once blur the joke and make the clip impossible to follow.
- **Principle: heightening.**
  Use: build escalation into the card in 2–3 steps (sing to the cashier, then get the cashier to sing one word back, then get a bystander to hum along), with points for each step.
  Fails: escalation that ends in pressure on the stranger ("don't leave until they agree") turns heightening into harassment. The escalation must be in the team's own commitment, and every step the stranger takes must be optional.
- **Principle: commitment and deadpan.**
  Use: score "not breaking," meaning the team plays it totally straight (a news report about a pigeon, read as if it were breaking news). The contrast between the serious delivery and the silly content is the incongruity.
  Fails: teams who giggle and wink the whole time get rid of the contrast, and the clip shows kids embarrassed rather than a bit.
- **Principle: "Yes, and" with the stranger.**
  Use: give the team a script that accepts and builds on whatever the stranger says ("You don't know where the Knesset is? Perfect, then you'll be our expert on where it ISN'T"), so a stranger's "no" becomes part of the scene.
  Fails: a mission that only succeeds with one specific answer from the stranger pushes the team to pressure them.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed study that measures "commitment to the bit" or deadpan as a cause of laughter; this section rests on comedy-craft sources.
- I did not access the UCB Comedy Improv Manual directly.

## 3. Social psychology of strangers: why strangers say yes and enjoy it; low-cost asks; consent and opt-out

### Takeaway
People strongly underestimate how much strangers enjoy short interactions, and how much those strangers like them. So the main barrier for a team is their own fear, not the stranger's refusal. Design asks that are short, clear, easy to refuse, and that make the stranger the hero, not the target.

### Cited Findings
- Epley & Schroeder (2014, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*, "Mistakenly Seeking Solitude"): commuters told to talk to a stranger on a Chicago-area train reported a more positive ride than those told to sit in silence, yet beforehand most predicted the opposite. — [Discover Magazine](https://discovermagazine.com/mind/youd-be-happier-if-you-talked-to-strangers-more-often); [NPR/KUER](https://www.kuer.org/2014-12-02/study-shows-riding-the-quiet-car-is-crushing-your-spirit)
- Participants estimated that fewer than half of fellow commuters would want to talk, yet none reported being snubbed. The paper reports nine experiments, and in a waiting-room version both the person who started the conversation and the person spoken to felt more positive. (Secondary coverage, check the original for exact numbers.) — [NPR/KUER](https://www.kuer.org/2014-12-02/study-shows-riding-the-quiet-car-is-crushing-your-spirit); [Juliana Schroeder's site](https://julianaschroeder.com/news/tag/mistakenly+seeking+solitude)
- NPR coverage noted that the benefit also held for more introverted people. — [NPR/KUER](https://www.kuer.org/2014-12-02/study-shows-riding-the-quiet-car-is-crushing-your-spirit)
- Sandstrom, Boothby & Cooney: across seven studies people expected many fears (not enjoying the conversation, not liking the partner, poor social skills), and few came true. People worried more that the partner would not enjoy it than that they themselves would not. — [Essex repository](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/28669/); [SPSP blog](https://www.spsp.org/news-center/blog/sandstrom-boothby-strangers-conversations)
- Sandstrom, Boothby & Cooney (2022, *JESP*, "Talking to strangers: a week-long intervention reduces psychological barriers to social connection"): participants given "missions" to start conversations with strangers found them far more pleasant than they expected. — [SPSP blog](https://www.spsp.org/news-center/blog/sandstrom-boothby-strangers-conversations); [Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/platonic-love/202304/why-you-should-talk-to-strangers)
- Boothby, Cooney, Sandstrom & Clark (2018, *Psychological Science*, "The Liking Gap in Conversations"): after conversations, people underestimated how much their partner liked them. This held for lab strangers, college dormmates and adults at a workshop. Outside coders could see the liking, so the signals were there but people did not notice them. — [Wikipedia: Liking gap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liking_gap); [Essex repository](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/23025/); [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/illusion-chasers/people-like-you-more-than-you-know)
- Improv Everywhere (public-space prank group): their stated ethos is "positive pranks" that surprise and delight but never humiliate or embarrass the people who receive them. Charlie Todd says the best projects "invite people in" rather than force themselves on people, and that confusing or surprising someone is fine while embarrassing them is not. — [TVRev interview](https://www.tvrev.com/news/charlie-todd-director-improv-everywhere-big-block-interview); [CBS New York](https://www.cbsnews.com/amp/newyork/news/snapshot-new-york-improv-everywhere-videos); [ABC News](https://abcnews.go.com/WN/story?id=4784708)
- Counterpoint: some of Improv Everywhere's early conflict-based bits (a fake hypnotism in Central Park) angered unsuspecting bystanders. — [Pajiba review of "We Cause Scenes"](https://www.pajiba.com/film_reviews/we-cause-scenes-review-the-splendid-art-of-the-prank.php)

### Inferences (applied to missions)
- **Principle: the fear is miscalibrated.**
  Use: tell teams outright in the briefing that "most people enjoy this more than you think," and make the first stranger mission a very easy one (ask for a high five, ask for a recommendation) to break the ice.
  Fails: if the first stranger mission is the hardest, an early refusal confirms the fear and the team avoids strangers for the rest of the night.
- **Principle: low cost to the stranger.**
  Use: an ask under 30 seconds, with one clear action (say a word, judge between two options, pose), and no money, no personal data, no touching.
  Fails: long or vague asks ("help us with a mission") make strangers feel trapped.
- **Principle: the stranger as expert or judge, not as target.**
  Use: "Rate our performance from 1 to 10," "Which of the two of us looks more like a news anchor?," "What's the best falafel within 200 m?" These give the stranger status and an easy yes.
  Fails: missions where the stranger is fooled, confused on purpose, or filmed reacting to something strange.
- **Principle: explicit opt-out.**
  Use: the opening line is a fixed script ("We're in a city race, can we ask you something silly for 10 seconds? It's fine to say no"), and a "no" earns the team a small consolation (for example, they must thank the person dramatically and leave), so there is no incentive to push.
  Fails: when points depend only on the stranger saying yes, teens push. The scoring itself creates the harassment.
- **Principle: filming consent.**
  Use: film the team, not the stranger. A stranger appears in a clip only after a clear OK, and never minors without a parent there.
  Fails: secret filming of a stranger's reaction is the classic prank-video format, and it is exactly what turns "troll" into cruel (and, in Israel, can raise privacy issues; not researched here).
- **Principle: who you approach.**
  Use: open, relaxed places (a market, a busy promenade, people waiting in line).
  Fails: people in a hurry, people alone at night, people with small children, workers who are busy during a rush, and security guards. Also, a pack of six teenagers surrounding one adult at night is threatening whatever the mission says; limit it to 1–2 team members approaching while the rest stand back.

### Gaps
- No found research on strangers' reactions to TEENAGERS (as opposed to adults) approaching them, or at night specifically.
- Israeli law on filming people in public spaces and publishing it was not covered; it needs a separate check.
- No direct research found on refusal rates for silly requests to strangers.

## 4. Embarrassment and teens: sensitivity to social evaluation, group vs. individual embarrassment, keeping "cringe" fun

### Takeaway
Adolescents are biologically at the peak of sensitivity to being watched, and peers both raise embarrassment and push teens toward risk. This is a two-sided tool: the same audience that makes a mission thrilling can make it humiliating or dangerous. Spread the embarrassment across the group, give choice, and use personas as a mask.

### Cited Findings
- Somerville et al. (2013, *Psychological Science*): 69 participants aged 8 to almost 23 were told a peer was watching them through a camera (there was no real camera). Self-reported embarrassment, physiological arousal, and medial prefrontal cortex activation all peaked in adolescence, and autonomic arousal was uniquely higher in adolescents. — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3742683/); [APS press release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/?p=87345)
- The authors suggest that the MPFC–striatum pathway may link social evaluation to motivated behavior, including teens' riskier choices around peers. This is a hypothesis, not a proof. — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3742683/)
- Gardner & Steinberg (2005): in a computerized driving game, risk-taking dropped with age, and playing with three peers present raised risk-taking much more in adolescents and young adults than in adults. Peers also raised stated preference for risk. (Secondary sources; the specific figure of "306 participants / roughly double" could not be verified.) — [Frontiers in Psychology review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01476/full); [LiveScience](https://www.livescience.com/culture/teens-friends-take-risks-110131.html)
- Krach et al. (2011, *PLoS One*, "Your Flaws Are My Pain"): watching others embarrass themselves activates regions linked to empathy for pain (anterior cingulate, left anterior insula), whether the person acted on purpose or by accident, and whether or not they knew they were in an awkward spot. The strength correlated with trait empathy. — [University of Lübeck research portal](https://research.uni-luebeck.de/en/publications/your-flaws-are-my-pain-linking-empathy-to-vicarious-embarrassment/); [Neurosynth record](https://neurosynth.org/studies/21533250)

### Inferences (applied to missions)
- **Principle: peak sensitivity to being watched.**
  Use: the most exposing missions come later in the night, after the team has warmed up, and the camera points at the team they agreed to be on, not at one chosen kid.
  Fails: forcing a quiet kid into a solo performance in the first minutes, when Somerville-style evaluation pressure is highest.
- **Principle: shared embarrassment is lighter.**
  Use: whole-team performances (all six do the slow-motion run, all sing the jingle), where nobody stands out alone.
  Fails: "the youngest one does it" or "the team chooses a victim" create one scapegoat and invite bullying inside the team.
- **Principle: choice and volunteering.**
  Use: give 2–3 options of different "exposure levels" for the same points (sing / dance / make a speech), and let the team pick who does what.
  Fails: one mandatory way of doing the mission means the most self-conscious kid either suffers or blocks the team.
- **Principle: personas as a mask (distance, which links back to BVT).**
  Use: give the team a character ("you are a delegation from the Ministry of Hummus checking quality standards"). The embarrassment belongs to the character, not to the kid.
  Fails: missions that ask about the kid's own real life ("tell a stranger your biggest secret") remove the mask.
- **Principle: peers push toward risk.**
  Use: "dare" scaling should reward creativity and commitment, never physical risk (heights, traffic, running across roads, climbing, entering places).
  Fails: an open-ended "the more extreme, the more points" design will, according to Gardner & Steinberg, produce the riskiest behavior exactly when friends are watching, which is all night long.
- **Principle: vicarious cringe.**
  Use: a little cringe in the clip is engaging (the audience squirms along), so it is fine for the team to look ridiculous on purpose.
  Fails: when the viewer feels the person is really in pain or unaware, cringe turns into pity, and the clip stops being funny.

### Gaps
- No research found specifically on group vs. individual embarrassment in teens (the "shared is lighter" inference is not directly sourced).
- No research found on role-play or personas reducing embarrassment in teenagers specifically.

## 5. Game design for teams: choice, risk/reward, catch-up, rivalry, emergent stories, constraints (Taskmaster), the magic circle

### Takeaway
The "magic circle" is the reason a group of teens can act ridiculously in public: a game creates a temporary space with its own rules that people enter voluntarily. In a city race that circle leaks into public space, so the design has to make the circle visible to outsiders. Creative constraints (the Taskmaster style) beat open "be funny" prompts.

### Cited Findings
- Huizinga (*Homo Ludens*) describes play-grounds (the arena, the card table, the magic circle, the stage) as temporary worlds inside ordinary life with their own special rules. — [Wikipedia: Magic circle (games)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magic_circle_(games))
- Salen & Zimmerman (*Rules of Play*, 2004) turned this into a design concept: the special place in time and space a game creates. Per Stenros, their version is entered voluntarily, has rules different from ordinary life, and its boundaries are fuzzy and permeable. — [Stenros, "In Defence of a Magic Circle" (2012)](https://mycourses.aalto.fi/pluginfile.php/1259516/course/section/158265/Stenros%20-%20In%20Defence%20of%20a%20Magic%20Circle%20%282012%29.pdf); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magic_circle_(games))
- Pervasive games, which use the real built environment as play space, break both the spatial and the temporal limits of the magic circle. — [Montola-tradition DiGRA paper](https://dl.digra.org/index.php/dl/article/download/359/359); [Univ. of Suffolk: "Off the board"](https://oars.uos.ac.uk/1157/)
- Patricia Stokes (*Creativity from Constraints*, 2006): constraints that rule out the familiar, tried-and-true response while pushing toward novel responses produce creative breakthroughs. Constraints come in pairs (one rules something out, one points somewhere new). — [Scott Barry Kaufman, "Does creativity require constraints?"](https://scottbarrykaufman.com/does-creativity-require-constraints); [Creativity Post](https://www.creativitypost.com/article/does_creativity_require_constraints)

### Inferences (applied to missions)
- **Principle: a visible magic circle.**
  Use: a visible marker of being in the game (shirt, sign, card, or a fixed opening line) tells outsiders "this is a game," which is the alternative norm from section 1.
  Fails: teams with nothing visible doing strange things at night look like a threat or a drunk group, not a game.
- **Principle: the circle is entered voluntarily (Stenros).** Players agreed to it, strangers did not. Use: strangers come into the circle only by a short invitation they can refuse. Fails: treating everyone on the street as an NPC in your game.
- **Principle: paired constraints (Stokes / Taskmaster).**
  Use: one constraint rules out the obvious and one opens a new direction ("Get a stranger to say the word 'pineapple' without saying it yourself, and without asking a question").
  Fails: "do something funny with a stranger" with no constraint gives the most obvious, often crude, answer; too many constraints feel impossible.
- **Principle: meaningful choice, risk/reward.** Use: a "safe vs. bold" card (10 points for the gentle version, 25 for the bold one that is still benign), or a bet on yourself before the attempt ("we'll get 3 strangers to sing"), where over-betting costs points. Fails: when the "bold" version means more pressure on a stranger instead of more commitment from the team.
- **Principle: catch-up.** Use: comedy missions with points for creativity, judged by a person, are a good catch-up tool because speed does not decide them. Fails: if scoring is fully subjective and opaque, teams feel the judging is unfair. Publish clear criteria (commitment, creativity, stranger smiled).
- **Principle: rivalry and emergent stories.** Use: missions that leave traces for other teams to find ("leave a sticky note with a compliment for the next team"), and a callback at the finish where the best clips are screened. Fails: rivalry missions that sabotage another team (hiding things, misleading them) make the night tense rather than funny.

### Gaps
- No source found for Jesse Schell's "lenses" or for catch-up/rubber-banding mechanics in this pass; that is general game-design practice and the points above are unsourced inference.
- No research or primary source found on how Taskmaster designs its tasks (only Stokes' general theory).

## 6. Virality: Berger & Milkman and STEPPS applied to short challenge clips

### Takeaway
Content spreads when it causes HIGH arousal (awe, excitement, amusement; and also anger or anxiety), not just positive feeling. For a race, the goal is the positive high-arousal side: surprise, amusement, "I can't believe they did that." The share should make the sharer look good (social currency).

### Cited Findings
- Berger & Milkman (2012, *Journal of Marketing Research*, "What Makes Online Content Viral?"): looking at about 7,000 New York Times articles, content that causes high-arousal positive emotion (awe) or negative emotion (anger, anxiety) was more viral, and low-arousal emotion (sadness) was less viral. Positive content spread more overall, but arousal mattered beyond valence. — [the paper's PDF](https://cssh.northeastern.edu/pandemic-teaching-initiative/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2020/09/What-Makes-Online-Content-Viral.pdf); [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/the-secret-to-online-success-what-makes-content-go-viral)
- The results held after controlling for how surprising, interesting or useful the content was, and for how prominently it was placed; experiments showed that the emotion's activation level caused the sharing. — [the paper's PDF](https://cssh.northeastern.edu/pandemic-teaching-initiative/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2020/09/What-Makes-Online-Content-Viral.pdf)
- Berger's STEPPS (*Contagious*, 2013): Social currency, Triggers, Emotion, Public, Practical value, Stories. Not all six are needed; the more the better. Summaries describe high-arousal emotions as including awe, excitement and humor. (Secondary summaries, not the book text.) — [Shortform](https://www.shortform.com/blog/stepps-marketing/); [Penn Today interview with Berger](https://penntoday.upenn.edu/2013-04-18/interviews/qa-jonah-berger); [Actionable Books](https://actionablebooks.com/summaries/contagious-2)

### Inferences (applied to missions)
- **High arousal.** Use: missions with a clear moment of surprise or a peak (the stranger suddenly joins in, the whole team freezes at once). Fails: calm or "cute" missions (low arousal) are pleasant but are not shared; and arousal through anger (a rude prank) does spread, but it spreads as outrage at the brand and the kids.
- **Social currency.** Use: the clip makes the team look brave and creative, and makes the stranger look like a good sport, so both want to share it. Fails: a clip where someone looks foolish against their will; that person will not share it, and others will share it as criticism.
- **Triggers.** Use: tie missions to things Jerusalem teens see every day (the light rail, falafel stands, the shuk) so seeing them again brings the clip back to mind. Fails: one-off settings nobody runs into again.
- **Public.** Use: a uniform look (the same shirt or sign in every clip) makes the event recognizable across many clips. Fails: clips that look like random teens; nobody knows it is the same event.
- **Stories.** Use: a 3-beat structure built into the mission (setup, the ask, the reaction), suited to a 15–30 second clip. Fails: a 2-minute clip with no clear peak.
- **Practical value** is mostly irrelevant for funny clips; do not force it.

### Gaps
- Berger & Milkman measured news articles, not short videos; applying the findings to TikTok/Reels-style clips is an inference.
- No research found on virality of challenge or prank clips specifically, or on teen audiences.

## Summary checklist for the mission writer (inferences, combining the above)
1. One unusual thing inside a real everyday frame (UCB).
2. The violation lands on the team's own dignity, never on the stranger's (BVT and Improv Everywhere).
3. A visible magic circle and a fixed opening line that includes "it's fine to say no" (magic circle and consent).
4. The stranger as judge or expert, under 30 seconds, no touching, no personal data (Epley/Sandstrom).
5. Points never depend only on the stranger saying yes; a "no" has a fun exit.
6. Embarrassment shared by the team, with a choice of exposure level, and a persona as a mask (Somerville).
7. "Bold" means more commitment, never more physical risk or more pressure on a stranger (Gardner & Steinberg).
8. Two constraints: one that rules out the obvious and one that points somewhere new (Stokes).
9. A built-in peak moment for a 15–30 s clip, filming the team, not strangers without consent (Berger & Milkman).
10. Avoid norms Jerusalem holds strongly: religion, mourning, security, holy sites, and identity groups.
