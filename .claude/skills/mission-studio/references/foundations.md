# Foundations: why teens engage

The research in one page. Full text and sources: `docs/teen-missions-research-2026-10-20.md`.
Each principle ends with what it means for a mission.

1. **Embarrassment peaks at 14 to 15** (Somerville et al. 2013, fMRI; arousal peaked at 14.4).
   → Performing on command for strangers hits the most sensitive spot of our audience.
2. **Friends raise the reward, not just the risk** (Gardner & Steinberg 2005; Chein et al.
   2011). → The team is the audience. **Daring inside the team: yes. Exposure to strangers
   without a role: no.**
3. **Respect and status, not lessons** (Yeager, Dahl & Dweck 2018). Nielsen Norman on teens:
   "simple, not childish"; "boredom is the kiss of death". → Real difficulty, adult register,
   and a way to beat the mission by being clever.
4. **Choice** (Van Petegem et al. 2015: controlling requests produce defiance; leisure research:
   self-chosen activity was the strongest predictor of not being bored; teens are bored 30% of
   the time in clubs). → Give real choices, or at least freedom in how.
5. **Challenge matched to skill** (flow; Shernoff et al. 2003: engagement highest when challenge
   and skill are both high, in group work). → Ramp difficulty through the game.
6. **Curiosity is an information gap** (Loewenstein 1994). → Titles and setups that open a
   question.
7. **Visible individual contribution stops loafing** (Ringelmann; Williams, Harkins & Latané
   1981). → Each person brings a part you can see in the result.
8. **Competition is a young motivation** (Quantic Foundry: it declines most with age). → "Us
   against them" inside the mission, not only on the final board.
9. **People remember the peak and the end** (Kahneman). → A game needs one moment people retell,
   and an ending they hold in their hands.
10. **Constraints breed creativity** (Haught-Tromp 2017). → Never "be creative"; give a hard
    formal constraint.

From the escape room enthusiast survey (2017, Nicholson's taxonomy): best liked skills are
connecting clues for an "aha" (61%), noticing what matters (59%), pattern and logic (about 51%);
least liked are memorisation (14%), brute forcing codes and audio puzzles (11%). **Outside
knowledge (trivia) was the least accepted trope.** Physical challenges were the second most
wanted element. Favourite themes include spy and heist.

From the 1930s youth movement games (Moshe "Michel" Horowitz): categories of observation,
memory (Kim), estimation (אומדנא: height, quantity, distance), tracking, senses, agility, relays;
and the rule **no game is distributed before rounds of trial and correction with real kids**.
